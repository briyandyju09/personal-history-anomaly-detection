How much personal history is enough?

Measuring the data requirement for individualised financial behavioural anomaly detection.

300 real bank accounts 300 synthetic card entities (E7) 240 simulated individuals 1,056,320 real transactions 5-seed replication seed 20260920
Read this first. An anomaly is a deviation from a behavioural baseline. It is not fraud. This study produces no evidence about fraud, and nothing here should be used to justify adverse action against a customer. Every number on this page was produced by executed code; nothing is estimated or illustrative — except the "Meet the user" and "Mystery transaction" demos near the end, which are clearly-labelled constructed teaching examples, not data.

The question

How many purchases do we need to see before a computer can reliably learn what "normal spending" looks like for one person — and does that number even exist, or does it depend on the person?

A personalised anomaly detector compares each transaction against a model of one person's own past. Per-user behavioural profiling for card fraud is a mature idea in the literature (e.g. Dal Pozzolo et al., 2018), and appears in deployed systems too. At the moment an account opens there is no past. Somewhere between zero transactions and a lifetime of them, the personal baseline becomes worth using.

Production systems handle this with a cutoff. One patent family describes filtering out "unseasoned users, i.e. users with insufficient history." The cutoff is treated as a known engineering parameter. We could not find published work measuring it.

Dataset

Two layers, kept strictly separate, plus a controlled simulation. Nothing from the synthetic layers is ever presented as a real-world observation, and nothing is pooled across layers.

Real data: PKDD'99 Financial ("Berka")

  • Source. Prepared by Petr Berka and Marta Sochorova for the PKDD'99 Discovery Challenge. Primary description: sorry.vse.cz/~berka/challenge/pkdd1999 (unreachable from our execution environment; retrieved via a GitHub mirror, cross-checked byte-for-byte against a second independent mirror — see Reproducibility).
  • License. Released for an open research challenge; no machine-readable licence on the primary page. Treated as research-use data; no raw records redistributed.
  • Fields. account_id, day-resolution date, credit/debit type, one of 5 operation modes, amount, balance (excluded from features — see paper §6.3), one of 7 k_symbol characterisations, partner bank/account.

Transactions per account, all 4,500 accounts, log-scaled bins. The long right tail is why history sizes above 250 aren't usable on this dataset without shrinking the evaluated cohort to an unrepresentative sliver (only 119 accounts have 500+).

Synthetic replication layer: IBM TabFormer

  • Source. github.com/IBM/TabFormer (Apache-2.0), data/credit_card/transactions.tgz. 24,386,900 transactions, 2,000 users, independently counted this session.
  • Entity unit. (User, Card), not User alone — 6,139 pairs, 4,538 with ≥ 560 transactions, supporting history sizes to 500.
  • Verification. Single-source only (no second independent mirror was fetched) — weaker provenance than Berka's dual-mirror check, stated as a limitation.

Controlled simulation

240 individuals across six behavioural archetypes (40 each), each defined by a measurable parameter vector: log-amount dispersion, merchant count and Zipf exponent, category count, location count and home-location weight, inter-arrival Gamma shape, hour-of-day concentration, drift rate. Used where real data cannot supply merchant/category/geography channels or controlled variability.

Shaded bands are 95% bootstrap confidence intervals over accounts (2,000 resamples). Dashed line is the population model, which uses no personal data. Prevalence baseline is 0.100. Switch the metric row to see precision, recall, F1 or false-positive rate instead of PR-AUC.

Three regimes. Mahalanobis and the autoencoder rise steeply and saturate early. k-NN is flat or declining to 25 transactions, then jumps. Both univariate z-scores on transaction amount are flat from 1 to 250 — they never learn anything at all.

Saturation fits

Four of six curves are well described by a saturating exponential. The Gaussian-z fit has R² = 0.469 and a nonsensical y₀; we report it and decline to interpret it, because a saturation model is not the right description of a flat curve. This is why R² is reported rather than parameters alone.

2. Ranking quality and the operating point (corrected)

Correction. The first draft of this paper reported that a model which ranks well at 10 transactions "flags 96% of everything", and explained it as a noisy threshold estimate. A follow-up experiment (E9) found that figure is an artefact of calibrating the threshold on in-sample scores, which are biased low by construction. Both versions are shown below; only the leave-one-out one is a statement about a detector that could be deployed.

PR-AUC measures ranking. A deployed detector also needs an operating point: a threshold at the 95th percentile of the model's scores on its own training history, with no access to test labels.

First draft: threshold from in-sample training scores (biased)

Shrinkage Mahalanobis, personal arm. A model fitted on its own training points scores those same points as unusually normal, so the 95th-percentile threshold lands far too low. For n ≤ 21 (the feature count) the covariance is singular and this is most severe.

Corrected: threshold from leave-one-out training scores (Experiment E9)

Each training point is scored by a model fitted on the other n−1. Same 300 accounts, seed and injected labels as above. Nominal FPR is 0.05. Mahalanobis, primary seed. Leave-one-out has no score at n=1, so nothing is flagged there. The next panel repeats this for every detector across five seeds.

Every detector, five cohort seeds (E9 full run)

Mean false-positive rate under leave-one-out calibration, with the in-sample value in parentheses. Nominal is 0.05. 300 accounts × 5 seeds per cell; exact leave-one-out refits for every detector. The in-sample rows reproduce the earlier five-seed results to within 1e-16.

Recall under leave-one-out calibration, same cells.

With out-of-sample calibration the failure at 10 transactions is the opposite one: the Mahalanobis detector is almost silent (it flags 1.2% and catches 5.8% of the anomalies), not noisy. From 25 transactions its false-positive rate is 0.066 against a 0.05 target, so for the detectors that also rank well (Mahalanobis, the autoencoder, the hybrid) a usable operating point appears at about the same history size as the ranking threshold, not at 50. Not every detector follows: k-NN is silent until about 50, Isolation Forest does not reach a useful recall within 250, and the z-score detectors stay conservative and weak. The bias is also not uniform in direction: for Isolation Forest the out-of-sample rule flags more than the in-sample one at n=5, which may be a scale mismatch between the n−1-point forest and the n-point forest (untested). The hybrid has the best small-history operating behaviour measured (n=10: FPR 0.028, recall 0.254, against 0.012 and 0.055 for personal Mahalanobis), an observation, not a significance-tested comparison. Two caveats: the 5% target is still missed by 1.6–3 points at most sizes, and leave-one-out is only one out-of-sample rule (split-half and population-derived thresholds were not compared). What survives: PR-AUC alone does not say whether an operating point is usable, and the operating point depends heavily on how the threshold is calibrated.

3. Personalisation threshold explorer

Pick how good you want the model to be — say, "at least 90% as good as it'll ever get" — and this shows how many transactions that requires. Different goals give different answers, which is the point: there's no single magic number.

A "personalisation threshold" is not one quantity. Move the slider to see how the relative criterion — the smallest history reaching a given fraction of the model's best observed performance, and holding it — changes the answer.

Criterion: 90% of best observed performance

Note the failure mode: a relative criterion rewards models that never improve. Robust z reaches 90% of its own (worthless) best at 1 transaction. A relative threshold answers "when does this model stop improving", not "when is this model any good".

The stability criterion is undefined for every model that works. Across-account standard deviation never falls below its own median for Mahalanobis, k-NN, the autoencoder or Isolation Forest, at any history size. The mean curve hides enormous individual variation that does not shrink with more data. A system meeting a target on average will still fail badly for a substantial minority of users.

4. Personal, population, and hybrid

The hybrid shrinks personal estimates toward population estimates with a weight determined only by history size:

μ = (n·μ_personal + κ·μ_global) / (n + κ), κ = 25 fixed in advance

At n = 0 this is exactly the population model; as n grows it converges to the personal model.

Paired over 300 accounts. Personalisation is actively harmful below a model-dependent crossover: between 5 and 10 transactions for Mahalanobis, between 25 and 50 for k-NN. Below it, a model built from strangers' data beats the person's own history by large effect sizes.

The hybrid is far better than the personal model when history is scarce (+0.188 at n = 5) and indistinguishable from it beyond 25 (Holm-adjusted p ≥ 0.795). It is never worse than either parent at any size.

A contrary result we report because it qualifies the above. In the simulation the same hybrid plateaus at 0.401 while the personal model reaches 0.924. The simulation's strongest features are personal novelty indicators — is this merchant one you have used — and the hybrid computes them against the population reference, destroying exactly the signal that matters. Parameter shrinkage cannot recover information the feature representation has already discarded. Hybrids help when personal signal lives in the parameters of a shared representation, not when it lives in the representation itself.

5. Behavioural complexity: a controlled result and a real-data null

Some people spend the same way every week; others spend all over the place — many shops, wildly different amounts, odd hours. Does the "wild" kind of person need more history before the computer can tell what's normal for them? We manipulated this directly in a simulation (yes, a lot) and then looked for the same pattern in real accounts (no detectable pattern).

The real dataset has no merchant, category or geography fields, and variability can only be observed, not manipulated. So we also simulated 240 individuals across six archetypes, each defined by a measurable parameter vector rather than a label.

Simulation only. Nothing here is a real-world observation.

The archetypes differ tenfold in data requirement while converging to similar asymptotes. Variability changes the rate of learning far more than the ceiling.

The same test on real accounts

Spearman correlations, Holm-corrected across the family of eight. No descriptor predicts an account's data requirement (all adjusted p ≥ 0.867, all |ρ| ≤ 0.12). We report the null rather than selecting an uncorrected result.

A partial test on card-structured data (Experiment E7, synthetic)

TabFormer has exactly the merchant/category/geography channels Berka lacks. On this synthetic dataset, state_entropy does significantly predict the threshold after Holm correction — unlike every descriptor on Berka. Mild evidence that the real-data null reflects missing channels rather than a genuinely absent effect; it does not resolve the question, since TabFormer is synthetic.

6. Anomaly classes have different data requirements

Mean anomaly-score percentile, simulation. 0.5 is chance.

Categorical-novelty anomalies (merchant, category, geography) start near chance and improve steeply, because recognising an unfamiliar merchant requires having seen the familiar ones. Amount and temporal anomalies start well above chance at one transaction and improve least. Amount detectability actually declines from 0.789 at n = 1 to 0.576 at n = 10 before recovering: apparent competence at n = 1 is an artefact of degenerate variance, not detection.

7. Count versus time — and what actually explains it

Our first version of this experiment was wrong. We widened temporal coverage by stride-sampling history, which changes the inter-arrival gap feature: under stride 2 the computed gaps double. It reported a large degradation (0.647 → 0.435). Most of that was a feature artefact. We recomputed gap features against each transaction's true position in the unthinned timeline and re-ran. The corrected effect is roughly six times smaller.

Transaction count held fixed at 50, 300 accounts.

A control arm we intended also failed, and we report it as such: with count held fixed, thinning by k necessarily reaches back k times further, so the control had the same span as the treatment. This is a logical property of the design, not a bug. Temporal coverage and sampling density cannot be varied independently at fixed count, so this question is not fully separable.

What can still be said: raising count from 50 to 250 at natural density changes performance from 0.647 to 0.642, essentially nothing, while holding count at 50 and drawing from a four-times-longer span costs 0.036. Recency dominates volume.

Does drift actually explain this? (Experiment E6 — tested directly)

Our first draft offered behavioural drift as an untested interpretation. We have since measured it directly: for each account, the distance between the 150 transactions added going from 100→250 history and the 100 already present, in the account's own recent coordinate system.

The drift interpretation does not survive direct measurement. Drift is real and grows with lag, but an account's own drift magnitude does not predict that account's own 100→250 marginal PR-AUC change. The mechanism behind that result is an open question again, not a settled interpretation.

8. Does the finding survive a different anomaly definition?

Under a ×5 amount-shift protocol the ordering changes: univariate Gaussian z, useless under the impostor protocol, becomes competitive, which is expected when the perturbation is applied to amount alone. Mahalanobis remains best at a lower level, and its relative-90 threshold is again 25. The threshold estimate is stable across two structurally different anomaly definitions; the achievable level is strongly protocol-dependent and should not be transferred between them.

9. Does the shape survive a different dataset? (Experiment E7, synthetic)

Every number below is from TabFormer, a synthetic dataset. Nothing here is a real-data observation, and nothing is pooled with the Berka results above.
Berka is one bank, one country, one decade, and has no information about which shop or what kind of shop a purchase was made at. A synthetic card-transaction dataset lets us check whether our findings are quirks of that one dataset, or whether they show up again on a very different kind of data — including channels (shop type, location) Berka doesn't have.

300 (User, Card) entities, history sizes to 500 (deeper than Berka permits).

One headline result does NOT replicate, and we say so plainly. On Berka, Isolation Forest is the worst detector at every history size. On TabFormer it is competitive-to-best at small history sizes — the single best detector at n = 5, 10, 25 and 50. The "sophisticated models can't help under scarcity" claim is dataset-dependent, not universal. We don't have a confirmed mechanism for the reversal and say so rather than inventing one.

Personal vs. global, Mahalanobis, TabFormer. The crossover (personalisation stops being harmful) falls later than on Berka — between n=25 and n=100 — because TabFormer's global model is itself much stronger.

10. Disparate-impact audit (Experiment E8)

The first draft of this paper argued that sparse-history users bear a disproportionate cost without auditing for it. We have since run an audit: does the personal detector's false-positive rate vary with an account's district-level average salary, unemployment rate, urbanisation or region? (Berka carries no individual-level demographic field — this is an ecological audit.)

No Holm-significant relationship on any of the four axes tested. The table above uses the first draft's in-sample FPR, which at n=10 sits at a ceiling (≈0.95 for everyone), so that half is uninformative. We repeated the audit with the corrected leave-one-out FPR (E8b, below): still nothing significant after correction. A weak negative salary trend (higher-salary districts, somewhat lower FPR; uncorrected p = 0.046 and 0.024 at two sizes, |ρ| ≤ 0.13) is reported but not claimed. With 300 accounts the audit cannot exclude weak effects. It also cannot test age, tenure, immigration status or disability, which Berka does not record.

Model comparison

We deliberately do not rank models overall. What the experiments show instead is which model behaves differently under which condition:

  • Under real-world scarcity (Berka): Mahalanobis best throughout; Isolation Forest worst throughout; k-NN needs ≥50 transactions before it does anything; univariate z-scores never learn.
  • Under synthetic card-structured scarcity (TabFormer): Isolation Forest reverses to best-at-small-n; Mahalanobis still wins at larger n.
  • Under population/personal competition: the global model wins below a crossover that is itself model- and dataset-dependent, never a fixed number.
  • Under hybridisation: the shrinkage hybrid dominates on Berka (real, parameter-shared signal) but underperforms the personal model in simulation (representation-level signal) — the same mechanism, opposite verdict, depending on where the personal information actually lives.

Limitations

  • One real dataset, one bank, one country, 1993-1998 retail banking. A second, synthetic, structurally different dataset (§9) probes but cannot close this.
  • Day-resolution timestamps on real data — no hour-of-day modelling possible there.
  • No merchant, category or geographic channels on real data — the anomaly classes with the steepest data requirements in simulation, and TabFormer's one significant complexity descriptor, could not be tested on real behaviour.
  • Real-data history stops at 250 because only 119 of 4,500 accounts have 500+ transactions.
  • The evaluated cohort is the more active half of the dataset, so it is least representative of the sparse-history users this study is most about.
  • Neither injection protocol models an adversary trying to imitate the account holder — the hardest and most important case.
  • The 10% injection rate is far above realistic fraud prevalence. Curve shape transfers; absolute values do not.
  • Cohort selection was repeated at 5 seeds for the primary curve (E5) — see §1 — but E2, Isolation Forest/autoencoder in the simulation, and E3/E4/E7/E8 remain single-seed.
  • Wilcoxon p values over 300 paired accounts are tiny for effects of any size, which is why effect sizes appear beside every test.
  • The disparate-impact audit (§10) is ecological (district-level); Berka has no individual demographic field to audit directly.
  • Operating-point calibration (E9). The first draft's threshold rule was biased at small n. Leave-one-out calibration covers the six personal detectors and the shrinkage hybrid on Berka at five seeds. Every other FPR / flag-rate figure on this site (the global arm, the score-fusion hybrid, the parametric protocol, the simulation, TabFormer) still uses the in-sample rule. That rule is biased low for most detectors at small n but is not an upper bound for all of them (Isolation Forest at n=5 goes the other way), so those figures are not a worst case either. PR-AUC-based results and thresholds do not depend on any threshold rule and are unaffected.
  • The relative-90 threshold is read off a coarse history-size grid: "25" means the first grid point reached, and the true crossing lies between 10 and 25.

Ethics

The transactions these detectors flag are, by construction, transactions that differ from a person's past. In real life those are holidays, house moves, medical costs, a new job, a death in the family, leaving an abusive relationship. A system that escalates deviation to suspicion punishes people for changing.

False positives and false negatives are not symmetric. A missed anomaly costs an institution money. A false positive can mean a declined card abroad, a frozen account during an emergency, or a customer treated as a suspect.

Sparse-history users bear the cost. They are disproportionately new customers, young people, recent immigrants and people re-entering formal banking — exactly the users with the thinnest personal baseline. The first draft said a personal model "flags almost everything" for them; that figure came from a biased in-sample threshold (§2) and is withdrawn. With out-of-sample calibration the thin-history failure at 10 transactions is a nearly silent detector, and from about 25 the false-positive rate is 6–8% against a 5% target. The concern about deploying personalisation without a history floor stands, but its form is less clear-cut than we first claimed. Our finding that a population or hybrid model beats personalisation in the sparse regime (by PR-AUC, which does not depend on the threshold rule) is unaffected.

Behavioural features are proxies for life circumstances. Irregular income, cash reliance and geographic mobility correlate with class, occupation, immigration status and disability. A detector calibrated on regular salaried behaviour treats irregular lives as anomalous by default. We have since run a disparate-impact audit (§10) and found no Holm-significant relationship between false-positive rate and district-level salary, unemployment, urbanisation or region, under either calibration rule (300 accounts cannot exclude weak effects) — which narrows, but does not clear, this concern: individual-level axes (age, tenure, immigration status, disability) remain untested, and no system of this kind should be deployed without auditing them.
