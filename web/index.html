How much personal history is enough?

Measuring the data requirement for individualised financial behavioural anomaly detection.

300 real bank accounts 240 simulated individuals 1,056,320 transactions seed 20260920
Read this first. An anomaly is a deviation from a behavioural baseline. It is not fraud. This study produces no evidence about fraud, and nothing here should be used to justify adverse action against a customer. Every number on this page was produced by executed code; nothing is estimated or illustrative.

The question

A personalised anomaly detector compares each transaction against a model of one person's own past. At the moment an account opens there is no past. Somewhere between zero transactions and a lifetime of them, the personal baseline becomes worth using.

Production systems handle this with a cutoff. One patent family describes filtering out "unseasoned users, i.e. users with insufficient history." The cutoff is treated as a known engineering parameter. We could not find published work measuring it.

1. Data-efficiency curves on real data

Each account's evaluation window is its last 60 transactions, fixed. Training history is the n transactions immediately before it. The window never changes, so comparisons across history sizes are paired within account and nothing is ever both trained on and tested on.

Ground truth is provenance, not a statistical rule: a held-out transaction is replaced by a real transaction from a different real account. No detector's score is involved in producing the label, so it cannot be circular.

Shaded bands are 95% bootstrap confidence intervals over accounts (2,000 resamples). Dashed line is the population model, which uses no personal data. Prevalence baseline is 0.100.

Three regimes. Mahalanobis and the autoencoder rise steeply and saturate early. k-NN is flat or declining to 25 transactions, then jumps. Both univariate z-scores on transaction amount are flat from 1 to 250 — they never learn anything at all.

Saturation fits

Four of six curves are well described by a saturating exponential. The Gaussian-z fit has R² = 0.469 and a nonsensical y₀; we report it and decline to interpret it, because a saturation model is not the right description of a flat curve. This is why R² is reported rather than parameters alone.

2. Ranking quality and deployability are not the same thing

PR-AUC measures ranking. A deployed detector needs an operating point. We set the threshold at the 95th percentile of the model's scores on its own training data — the rule a real system could apply, without touching test labels.

Shrinkage Mahalanobis, personal arm. At 10 transactions PR-AUC is 0.441, more than four times the prevalence baseline, and the same model flags 96% of everything. It achieves near perfect recall by alerting on almost all transactions.

With a short history the model cannot estimate the scale of its own score distribution even when it ranks tolerably well. The 95th percentile of ten training scores is a poor estimate of the 95th percentile of the score distribution. Ranking ability and calibration have different data requirements, and calibration is the more expensive of the two.

3. Threshold explorer

A "personalisation threshold" is not one quantity. Move the slider to see how the relative criterion — the smallest history reaching a given fraction of the model's best observed performance, and holding it — changes the answer.

Criterion: 90% of best observed performance

Note the failure mode: a relative criterion rewards models that never improve. Robust z reaches 90% of its own (worthless) best at 1 transaction. A relative threshold answers "when does this model stop improving", not "when is this model any good".

The stability criterion is undefined for every model that works. Across-account standard deviation never falls below its own median for Mahalanobis, k-NN, the autoencoder or Isolation Forest, at any history size. The mean curve hides enormous individual variation that does not shrink with more data. A system meeting a target on average will still fail badly for a substantial minority of users.

4. Personal, population, and hybrid

The hybrid shrinks personal estimates toward population estimates with a weight determined only by history size:

μ = (n·μ_personal + κ·μ_global) / (n + κ), κ = 25 fixed in advance

At n = 0 this is exactly the population model; as n grows it converges to the personal model.

Paired over 300 accounts. Personalisation is actively harmful below a model-dependent crossover: between 5 and 10 transactions for Mahalanobis, between 25 and 50 for k-NN. Below it, a model built from strangers' data beats the person's own history by large effect sizes.

The hybrid is far better than the personal model when history is scarce (+0.188 at n = 5) and indistinguishable from it beyond 25 (Holm-adjusted p ≥ 0.795). It is never worse than either parent at any size.

A contrary result we report because it qualifies the above. In the simulation the same hybrid plateaus at 0.401 while the personal model reaches 0.924. The simulation's strongest features are personal novelty indicators — is this merchant one you have used — and the hybrid computes them against the population reference, destroying exactly the signal that matters. Parameter shrinkage cannot recover information the feature representation has already discarded. Hybrids help when personal signal lives in the parameters of a shared representation, not when it lives in the representation itself.

5. Behavioural complexity: a controlled result and a real-data null

The real dataset has no merchant, category or geography fields, and variability can only be observed, not manipulated. So we also simulated 240 individuals across six archetypes, each defined by a measurable parameter vector rather than a label.

Simulation only. Nothing here is a real-world observation.

The archetypes differ tenfold in data requirement while converging to similar asymptotes. Variability changes the rate of learning far more than the ceiling.

The same test on real accounts

Spearman correlations, Holm-corrected across the family of eight. No descriptor predicts an account's data requirement (all adjusted p ≥ 0.867, all |ρ| ≤ 0.12). We report the null rather than selecting an uncorrected result.

Two caveats limit how strongly the null should be read. 117 of 300 accounts (39%) had no defined threshold at all, because their curve never reached and held 90% of their own best, so the correlation runs on a non-random better-behaved subset. And the descriptors do correlate with best achievable performance: accounts with more diverse usage are easier to protect (ρ ≈ 0.20), plausibly because a distinctive profile is easier to tell from an impostor's.

We cannot distinguish three explanations. The Berka descriptors may be too thin, since the dataset lacks exactly the channels that carry the simulation's effect. The real cohort's variability range may be too narrow. Or observed variability in real data may be confounded with account type and tenure in ways the simulation excludes by construction. The simulation result is not evidence that the effect exists in real data; it is evidence that the effect exists when the generative parameters are what the theory says they are.

6. Anomaly classes have different data requirements

Mean anomaly-score percentile, simulation. 0.5 is chance.

Categorical-novelty anomalies (merchant, category, geography) start near chance and improve steeply, because recognising an unfamiliar merchant requires having seen the familiar ones. Amount and temporal anomalies start well above chance at one transaction and improve least. Amount detectability actually declines from 0.789 at n = 1 to 0.576 at n = 10 before recovering: apparent competence at n = 1 is an artefact of degenerate variance, not detection.

7. Count versus time, and a correction we had to make

Our first version of this experiment was wrong. We widened temporal coverage by stride-sampling history, which changes the inter-arrival gap feature: under stride 2 the computed gaps double. It reported a large degradation (0.647 → 0.435). Most of that was a feature artefact. We recomputed gap features against each transaction's true position in the unthinned timeline and re-ran. The corrected effect is roughly six times smaller.

Transaction count held fixed at 50, 300 accounts.

A control arm we intended also failed, and we report it as such: with count held fixed, thinning by k necessarily reaches back k times further, so the control had the same span as the treatment. This is a logical property of the design, not a bug. Temporal coverage and sampling density cannot be varied independently at fixed count, so this question is not fully separable.

What can still be said: raising count from 50 to 250 at natural density changes performance from 0.647 to 0.642, essentially nothing, while holding count at 50 and drawing from a four-times-longer span costs 0.036. Recency dominates volume. This is consistent with behavioural drift, but we did not measure drift directly, so that is an interpretation rather than a finding.

8. Does the finding survive a different anomaly definition?

Under a ×5 amount-shift protocol the ordering changes: univariate Gaussian z, useless under the impostor protocol, becomes competitive, which is expected when the perturbation is applied to amount alone. Mahalanobis remains best at a lower level, and its relative-90 threshold is again 25. The threshold estimate is stable across two structurally different anomaly definitions; the achievable level is strongly protocol-dependent and should not be transferred between them.

9. Computational cost

Median fit-and-score time in milliseconds. At 250 transactions Isolation Forest costs 340× more than Mahalanobis while performing worst at every history size.

10. Limitations

  • One dataset, one bank, one country, 1993-1998 retail banking. Generalisation to modern card payments is untested.
  • Day-resolution timestamps. No merchant, category or geographic channels on real data — precisely the channels with the steepest data requirements in simulation.
  • Real-data history stops at 250 because only 119 of 4,500 accounts have 500+ transactions.
  • The evaluated cohort is the more active half of the dataset, so it is least representative of the sparse-history users this study is most about.
  • Neither injection protocol models an adversary trying to imitate the account holder — the hardest and most important case.
  • The 10% injection rate is far above realistic fraud prevalence. Curve shape transfers; absolute values do not.
  • Single seed for cohort selection. κ = 25 was fixed in advance, not tuned.
  • Wilcoxon p values over 300 paired accounts are tiny for effects of any size, which is why effect sizes appear beside every test.

11. Ethics

The transactions these detectors flag are, by construction, transactions that differ from a person's past. In real life those are holidays, house moves, medical costs, a new job, a death in the family, leaving an abusive relationship. A system that escalates deviation to suspicion punishes people for changing.

False positives and false negatives are not symmetric. A missed anomaly costs an institution money. A false positive can mean a declined card abroad, a frozen account during an emergency, or a customer treated as a suspect.

Sparse-history users bear the cost. They are disproportionately new customers, young people, recent immigrants and people re-entering formal banking — exactly the users for whom a personal model flags almost everything. Deploying personalisation without a history floor concentrates false positives on populations already more likely to face financial exclusion. Our finding that a population or hybrid model beats personalisation in the sparse regime is directly relevant: for these users, personalisation is not merely useless, it is harmful.

Behavioural features are proxies for life circumstances. Irregular income, cash reliance and geographic mobility correlate with class, occupation, immigration status and disability. A detector calibrated on regular salaried behaviour treats irregular lives as anomalous by default. We did not audit for disparate impact, and no system of this kind should be deployed without one.

12. What we did not do

  • Multi-seed repetition of cohort selection.
  • Any evaluation against confirmed fraud labels — no labelled dataset with per-user histories was available.
  • Adversarial anomalies designed to evade a personal baseline.
  • Replication on card-payment data to test whether curve shapes transfer.
  • Disparate-impact audit across account or demographic segments.
  • Direct measurement of behavioural drift.
