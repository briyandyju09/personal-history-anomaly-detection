evidence behind it. Entries are in the order the decisions were taken.

## 1. Literature audit and novelty verdict

**Question.** Has the proposed contribution already been covered?

**Searches run.** Personalised per-user anomaly detection in card transactions;
learning curves and data efficiency for anomaly detection; cold-start fraud
detection; sample-size effects on Isolation Forest and unsupervised detectors;
sequence and profile-based fraud modelling.

**What exists.**

- Per-user behavioural profiling for card fraud is mature. Individual
  behavioural baselines, per-user thresholds and profile deviation scoring are
  established, including in issued patents (US 11005864, US 11706234 on
  median/MAD personal profiles; US 11025653 and family, which explicitly filter
  out "unseasoned users" with insufficient history).
- Aggregated cardholder features and realistic evaluation protocols are
  established (Dal Pozzolo et al., 2018; Jurgovsky et al., 2018;
  Bahnsen et al., 2016; Lucas and Jurgovsky, 2020 survey).
- Cold start is studied, but at the **institution or market** level: transfer
  learning from a data-rich payment processor to a data-poor one. Graph-based
  cold-start work (SparseFraudNet, ACM TOIS) addresses new users via relational
  aggregation rather than by quantifying how much personal history is needed.
- Learning-curve methodology is well developed as a general tool
  (Viering and Loog, 2023; Mohr and van Rijn, 2022) and has been applied to
  per-user cold start in **recommender systems** (e.g. SPMC, which sweeps a cap
  on per-user interaction counts) and in **wearable sensing**.

**What does not exist, as far as the audit found.** A systematic measurement of
detector performance as a function of the number of transactions in one
person's own history, for unsupervised behavioural anomaly detection on
financial transactions, with an operational definition of a sufficiency
threshold and a matched global/personal/hybrid comparison at every history
size. The patent literature *assumes* a sufficiency threshold exists and filters
on it; nobody appears to have measured where it is or whether it exists.

**Verdict: proceed, with a narrowed claim.** The contribution is empirical and
methodological, not algorithmic. Every model used here is standard and off the
shelf. The claim is about the measurement, the protocol and the threshold
definitions, not about a new detector. Any stronger novelty claim would be
unsupportable, and the paper states this explicitly.

**Residual risk.** The audit used web search over open sources. Paywalled
venues were reachable only through abstracts. It remains possible that a
closely related study exists behind a paywall or in the industrial literature.

## 2. Dataset selection

**Requirement.** Per-entity identity, timestamps, enough history per entity to
reach history sizes in the hundreds, a permissive licence, real behaviour.

**Rejected.**

| Dataset | Reason for rejection |
|---|---|
| ULB `creditcardfraud` (Kaggle, mlg-ulb) | PCA-anonymised features, **no cardholder identifier**. Per-user histories cannot be constructed. Fatal for this study. |
| IEEE-CIS Fraud Detection | No reliable user identifier; the widely used "UID" is a reverse-engineered Kaggle artefact, not a ground-truth entity key. |
| IBM TabFormer credit card set | Has user ids, timestamps, merchants, MCC, location and fraud labels, and is large (24.4M rows). But it is **synthetic**, generated by IBM's simulator. Held as a future extension, not as the real-data layer. |
| Sparkov / `kartik2112/fraud-detection` | CC0 and well structured, but synthetic. Same reason. |
| PaySim, BankSim | Synthetic agent simulations; also shallow per-entity histories. |
| Bank Account Fraud (NeurIPS 2022) | Application-level records, not transaction sequences per person. |

**Selected: the PKDD'99 Financial Dataset ("Berka").**

- Source: <https://sorry.vse.cz/~berka/challenge/pkdd1999/berka.htm>, prepared
  by Petr Berka and Marta Sochorova for the PKDD'99 Discovery Challenge.
- Real, anonymised transactions from a Czech bank, 1993-01-01 to 1998-12-31.
- 1,056,320 transactions across 4,500 accounts. Verified in our own load.
- Median 208 transactions per account; 3,883 accounts have at least 100.
- Fields: account id, date, credit/debit type, operation mode, amount, balance,
  transaction characterisation (`k_symbol`), partner bank and account.

**Acquisition note, recorded because it matters for reproducibility.** The
primary host did not resolve from our execution environment. Files were taken
from a GitHub mirror (`Kusainov/czech-banking-fin-analysis`). We did not accept
the mirror on trust. `src/data_loader.py` verifies every one of the eight
tables against the record counts published on the primary source page and
raises `DataIntegrityError` on any mismatch. All eight matched exactly:
account 4500, client 5369, disp 5369, order 6471, trans 1056320, loan 682,
card 892, district 77. SHA-256 digests of the retrieved files are printed by
the loader and recorded in the reproducibility appendix.

**Licensing.** The dataset was released for an open research challenge and has
been redistributed for research and teaching for over two decades. The primary
page states no explicit machine-readable licence. We therefore treat it as
research-use data, use it only for research, and redistribute no raw records in
this repository. Anyone reproducing this work downloads it themselves.

## 3. Known limitations of the chosen dataset, accepted deliberately

These constrain what the study can claim, and are carried into the paper.

1. **No fraud labels.** The dataset has none. This is why the study is about
   behavioural anomalies and never claims fraud detection.
2. **Day-level timestamps.** No time of day, so hour-of-day behaviour cannot be
   modelled. Temporal features are limited to day-of-month, day-of-week and
   inter-arrival gaps.
3. **No merchant or category fields, no geography.** Behavioural texture is
   thinner than in modern card data. `operation` and `k_symbol` are the only
   categorical behaviour channels. Merchant and geographic anomaly classes from
   the original brief cannot be studied on this dataset and are moved to the
   controlled simulation.
4. **1990s Czech retail banking.** Standing orders, cash withdrawals and
   pensions dominate. Generalisation to contemporary card spending is an open
   question, not an assumption.
5. **Ceiling on history depth.** The maximum is 675 transactions and only 119
   accounts reach 500. See decision 5.

## 4. Ground truth: how anomalies are defined

The brief warns against circular evaluation. This was the single most important
design decision.

**Rejected approach.** Labelling anomalies by a statistical rule such as a
z-score cutoff, then evaluating detectors that compute something similar. That
measures agreement between two versions of the same rule, not detection.

**Adopted: Protocol I, impostor substitution (primary).** A held-out
transaction of the target account is replaced by a real transaction from a
different real account, keeping the target's timeline slot and taking the
impostor's amount, credit/debit type, operation and `k_symbol`. Ground truth is
**provenance**: the row came from a different person's real behaviour. No
statistical criterion is involved, so the label cannot be circular with respect
to any detector.

Substitution rather than insertion, because inserting rows would perturb the
surrounding inter-arrival gaps and let detectors exploit that artefact. The
cost is that timing carries no signal under Protocol I; timing is studied under
Protocol P and in the simulation instead. This is a real limitation, stated as
one.

**Adopted: Protocol P, parametric perturbation (secondary).** A held-out
transaction is replaced by a copy of itself with one raw attribute shifted by a
magnitude fixed in advance (amount scaled, operation swapped to an unused
level, or date moved). The perturbation is defined on the raw attribute, never
on a detector score.

**What neither protocol is.** Neither produces fraud. A Protocol I row is one
person's ordinary transaction relocated into someone else's history. Neither
protocol models an adversary who is actively trying to imitate the account
holder, which is the case that matters most in deployment and the case this
design cannot speak to.

## 5. Experimental design changes made against the brief

The brief specified some parameters that the data cannot support. Each change
is recorded with its reason.

**5a. History sizes stop at 250, not 500.** The brief asked for up to 500.
Supporting n = 500 plus a held-out evaluation window requires at least 560
transactions, which only about 60 accounts have, and those accounts are the
most active in the dataset, so the n = 500 point would be computed on a biased
and much smaller cohort than every other point. That breaks the paired design
and makes the curve non-comparable across its own x-axis. We therefore evaluate
n in {1, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100, 250} on a single fixed cohort. This is a
limitation of the dataset, not an assumption about where the threshold lies.

**5b. Fixed evaluation window, varying training history.** For each account the
evaluation window is the last 60 transactions. Training history for size n is
the n transactions immediately preceding that window. The window is identical
across every n, so comparisons across history sizes are paired within account
and no transaction is ever both trained on and tested on.

**5c. Global model trained on disjoint donor accounts.** Population information
comes from the earliest transactions of accounts that are not evaluated, so the
global model never sees an evaluated account's own data at any point in time.

**5d. Thresholds set from training scores, never from test labels.** The
operating point is the 95th percentile of the scores the fitted model assigns
to its own training data. Choosing a threshold on test labels would leak and
would also not correspond to anything deployable.

**5e. `balance` excluded from features.** It is an accumulated state variable,
not a behavioural choice, and under Protocol I a substituted row would carry a
balance discontinuity that reveals provenance for reasons unrelated to
behaviour. Including it would inflate every score through a protocol artefact.

**5f. No composite behavioural complexity index.** The brief allowed one only
with a defensible methodology. We could not justify a weighting across
quantities measured on different scales, so descriptors are reported
individually and correlated individually, with Holm correction across the
family.

**5g. Clustering methods dropped in favour of k-NN distance.** The brief listed
K-Means, DBSCAN and GMM. With n as small as 1 to 25, in a 23-dimensional
one-hot-heavy space, cluster counts and density parameters cannot be estimated
without tuning on data we do not have. Distance to the k-th nearest training
point is the representation-appropriate member of that family, needs no cluster
count, and degrades gracefully. Using all three regardless would have been
variety for its own sake, which the brief explicitly warns against.

**5h. Autoencoder implemented in NumPy.** PyTorch is not installed in the
execution environment. A 23-4-23 tanh autoencoder trained with full-batch Adam
is small enough to implement directly, which also makes the run fully
deterministic. The brief asked for a small network, not a large one.

## 6. Statistical approach

- Resampling is over **accounts**, the independent unit. Resampling
  transactions would treat rows from one account as independent and understate
  uncertainty.
- Paired bootstrap and Wilcoxon signed-rank for arm comparisons, since every
  arm is evaluated on the same accounts and the same windows.
- Rank-biserial correlation as the effect size, reported alongside every p
  value, because significance over hundreds of paired accounts is easy and
  uninformative on its own.
- Holm correction within each family of tests.
- Saturation curves are fitted and reported **with R²**, so a poor fit is
  visible. No curve is forced onto data that does not support one.
